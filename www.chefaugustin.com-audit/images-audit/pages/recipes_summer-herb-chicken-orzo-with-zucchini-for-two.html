30-Minute Summer Herb Chicken Orzo with Zucchini for Two

A 30-minute summer dinner for two: seared chicken with creamy orzo, ribbons of zucchini, and a triple dose of fresh basil, parsley, and chives — light, green, and done in one pan.

By Chef Augustin LefèvreJuly 29, 2026

Ingredients

  • boneless, skinless chicken breasts, patted dry2 (6 oz)
  • tsp Diamond Crystal kosher salt, plus more to taste1/2
  • tsp freshly ground black pepper1/4
  • Tbsp olive oil2
  • yellow onion, finely diced (about 3/4 cup)1 small
  • garlic, thinly sliced2 cloves
  • dried orzo pasta1 cup (6 oz)
  • dry white wine (or additional chicken broth)1/4 cup
  • low-sodium chicken broth, warm1 1/2 cups
  • halved cherry tomatoes (about 5 oz)1 cup
  • zucchini (about 8 oz), shaved into ribbons with a vegetable peeler1 medium
  • lightly packed mixed fresh herbs (roughly equal parts basil, flat-leaf parsley, and chives), roughly chopped, plus extra for garnish1 cup
  • Tbsp unsalted butter, cold1
  • Tbsp finely grated Parmesan cheese2
Tips & FAQSave

Instructions

  1. 11

    Season chicken breasts on both sides with salt and pepper.

  2. 22

    Heat olive oil in a 10-inch skillet over medium-high heat. Sear chicken until golden on both sides, about 3–4 minutes per side. Transfer to a plate — the chicken will not be fully cooked yet.

  3. 33

    Reduce heat to medium. Add diced onion and a pinch of salt and cook, stirring occasionally, until translucent, about 3 minutes. Add sliced garlic and cook until fragrant, 30 seconds.

  4. 44

    Stir in orzo and toast for 60 seconds, stirring constantly, until the pasta smells nutty and is flecked with golden spots.

  5. 55

    Pour in white wine and let it bubble for 30 seconds, stirring up any browned bits. Add warm chicken broth and cherry tomatoes, then increase heat to bring to a simmer.

  6. 66

    Nestle chicken breasts back into the pan, along with any accumulated juices. Cover, reduce heat to low, and cook for 10–12 minutes, or until the thickest part of the chicken reaches an internal temperature of 165°F / 74°C and the orzo is tender and has absorbed most of the liquid.

  7. 77

    Transfer chicken to a cutting board and let rest while you finish the orzo. Fold zucchini ribbons and fresh herbs into the orzo, then add the cold butter and stir until the orzo is creamy and the zucchini has just wilted, about 2 minutes. Off heat, stir in Parmesan. Slice chicken and return to the pan or plate atop the orzo. Garnish with extra herbs.

Article and tips

There’s a moment in late July when the basil on my windowsill threatens to take over the kitchen, and I know it’s time for this herbed orzo. The first time I made it, I had no lemon in the house — just three potted herbs and a zucchini the size of a rolling pin. That happy accident turned into the dish I make at least twice a month all summer long: tender seared chicken, creamy orzo, ribbons of barely wilted zucchini, and cherry tomatoes that burst into sweet little puddles. No lemon, no mozzarella — just a triple dose of basil, parsley, and chives that does the heavy lifting.

Most easy chicken dinners for two and orzo recipes lean on lemon and melted cheese for their brightness and heft. (I’ve made enough of those to respect them.) But when the produce at the market is so good it barely needs cooking, the opportunity isn’t for another creamy, cheesy bake — it’s for something greener, lighter on the palate, and perfumed with herbs that actually taste like summer. The gap I kept seeing in the top search results was exactly that: nobody was letting the herbs and zucchini be the stars. So here we are.

Why This Works

The technique is classic one-pan starch absorption — think risotto but less fussy. By toasting the orzo in the residual oil from searing the chicken, you coat each grain in fat and build a nutty foundation before any liquid goes in. Deglazing with white wine lemon chicken orzo for two pulls those browned chicken bits off the pan floor (the fond), infusing the entire dish with savory depth. Then the warm broth and tomatoes simmer everything to a creamy finish — no cream needed, because orzo releases its starch into the liquid as it cooks, creating a natural, clinging sauce. The key is adding the zucchini ribbons and fresh herbs only at the very end. The residual heat from the hot orzo wilts the zucchini just enough so it retains a slight bite, while the herbs barely warm, releasing their volatile oils without turning bitter. It’s the difference between a dinner that tastes alive and one that tastes cooked-to-death.

Toasted orzo is the difference between porridge and a proper pasta dish — don’t skip those sixty seconds.

zo is done. Orzo is your starch and your sauce in one; I buy it in bulk because it’s the fastest, most forgiving pasta shape for weeknight one-pans. Zucchini gets shaved into long, thin ribbons with a simple vegetable peeler — they melt into the dish without turning to mush. Cherry tomatoes bring a pop of sweetness that balances the herbs. As for those herbs: basil, flat-leaf parsley, and chives in equal measure. If you can only find two of the three, the dish still sings, but when all three are in the mix, the basil gives you that unmistakable anise-adjacent perfume, the parsley adds a clean, grassy base note, and the chives lend a whisper of onion without any raw bite. A splash of dry white wine deglazes the pan (use additional chicken broth if you’re out of wine — it’ll still taste fantastic), and a tablespoon of cold butter stirred in at the end is the restaurant trick for a glossy, velvety finish. A dusting of Parmesan at the end ties it all together without weighing anything down.

What Most Recipes Get Wrong

Scan the first page of results and you’ll see a theme: lemon, cream, mozzarella, repeat. There’s nothing wrong with those ingredients, but they mask rather than celebrate what makes a summer chicken orzo special. The dish is already rich from the starch and the seared chicken; adding cream and melted cheese can push it into heavy-winter-casserole territory. Lemon, for all its brightness, can dominate and flatten the delicate herbal notes. The real move is trusting the herbs themselves. Basil, parsley, and chives in generous amounts — a full cup lightly packed — bring complexity without acidity or fat. They perfume the orzo, each forkful tasting like the plant, not the pantry. If you’ve ever had pesto made moments before serving, you know the effect. This recipe captures that feeling without needing a blender.

Chef’s Tips & What I’ve Learned

Shave the zucchini into ribbons, not slices. A vegetable peeler creates long, thin strands that drape through the orzo like wide pasta noodles. They cook in the residual heat in under two minutes, whereas coins or half-moons take longer and tend to release more water, which can thin out your creamy orzo. The ribbons also create visual contrast — those green curls twisting through the pale pasta look intentionally rustic.

Fresh herbs are not negotiable here. Dried basil and parsley deliver a dusty shadow of their fresh counterparts, and dried chives are simply a no. If you must use dried in a pinch, fry them briefly in the butter at the very end to bloom whatever oils remain, but accept that the dish will taste fundamentally different. Buy the fresh herbs — they’re the entire point.

Butter at the end is the hidden gloss. Stirring in a tablespoon of cold butter off the heat (a technique known as monter au beurre) emulsifies with the starchy sauce and gives the orzo that glossy, restaurant-plate finish. It also rounds out any sharp edges from the wine without muting the herbs.

Bring the broth to a simmer before adding it. Cold liquid dropped into a hot pan shocks the healthy dinner ideas for two slows the cooking process, which can lead to uneven absorption. I heat my broth in a glass measuring cup in the microwave for 60 seconds before pouring it in. It’s a tiny step that pays off in a creamier, more consistent result.

Can I Make This Dish Ahead of Time?

Orzo is at its best right from the pan. As it sits, the pasta continues to absorb liquid and can become slightly gluey. That said, leftovers hold up surprisingly well for up to 3 days in the refrigerator if you stir in a splash of warm broth while reheating. The one thing I wouldn’t do is freeze it — orzo can turn mushy when thawed, and the zucchini ribbons will release water and lose all their texture. If you’re planning ahead, prep the ingredients (shave the zucchini, chop the herbs, dice the onion) and store them separately in the fridge. The active cook time is only about 20 minutes, so with mise en place done, dinner lands on the table fast.

Can I Use Chicken Thighs Instead of Breasts?

Absolutely — and thighs are actually more forgiving if you tend to overcook poultry. Use two boneless, skinless chicken thighs per person (about 8 ounces total), and sear them exactly the same way. They’ll need roughly the same amount of time to reach 165°F / 74°C, but thighs stay juicy even if you overshoot by a few degrees. The darker meat also brings a slightly richer flavor that stands up well to the herbs. The dish will taste a bit more savory, a bit more substantial, but no less summery.

What If I Don’t Have Zucchini?

Summer squash works identically — the flavor is a touch sweeter, but the texture is the same. Thin asparagus spears, cut into 2-inch pieces and blanched briefly, could also stand in, though they won’t ribbon. Just toss them in at the end with the herbs. The dish is designed to be flexible with whatever green vegetable looks best at the market.

How Should I Store and Reheat Leftovers?

Cool to room temperature, then transfer to an airtight container and refrigerate within 2 hours. Reheat gently in a skillet over medium-low heat with 2–3 tablespoons of chicken broth or water, stirring occasionally until warmed through. Microwave works in a pinch — 60 seconds at 50% power with a splash of liquid and a loose cover. Don’t reboil or the herbs will turn brown and bitter.

What to Serve Alongside?

This is a complete one-pan meal in itself, so I keep the table simple. A green salad tossed in a light vinaigrette cuts through the richness. Crusty bread for swiping up the last of the creamy orzo is always welcome. A crisp white wine — an unoaked Chardonnay or a Vermentino — matches the herbal notes without overwhelming them.

You’ll finish your last forkful as the evening light stretches long across the table, the plates streaked with herb-flecked sauce and a few stray tomato seeds. The kitchen smells like basil and garlic, but only just — this isn’t a dish that announces itself; it’s one that makes you pause and stop talking for a moment. Two empty plates, a pan that needs nothing more than a quick rinse, and the quiet satisfaction of a meal that felt both effortless and essential.
