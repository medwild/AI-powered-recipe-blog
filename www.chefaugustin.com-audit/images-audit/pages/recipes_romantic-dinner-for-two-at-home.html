Ingredients

  • olive oil1 tablespoon
  • yellow onion, finely diced1 small
  • garlic, minced2 cloves
  • ground beef8 ounces (225g)
  • crushed tomatoes1 can (14.5 ounces / 411g)
  • water1/2 cup (120ml)
  • Diamond Crystal kosher salt1/2 teaspoon
  • freshly ground black pepper1/4 teaspoon
  • frozen peas, thawed1/2 cup (60g)
  • dried ziti (or penne)4 ounces (115g)
  • whole-milk ricotta cheese1/2 cup (120g)
  • shredded low-moisture mozzarella cheese1 cup (100g)
  • freshly grated Parmesan cheese1/4 cup (25g)
  • Fresh basil leaves, for garnish
Tips & FAQSave

Instructions

  1. 11

    Preheat the Oven Set the oven to 375°F (190°C) with a rack in the upper-middle position. You want the heat to hit the top of the skillet closely for the final cheese melt, without being right against the broiler.

  2. 22

    Sweat the Aromatics Heat the olive oil in a 10-inch oven-safe skillet over medium heat. Add the diced onion and cook, stirring occasionally, until soft and translucent—about 5 minutes. Stir in the minced garlic and cook 30 seconds more until fragrant. (If the garlic browns, start over. Burnt garlic turns acrid, and there’s no undoing it in a one-pan dish.) until the internal temperature reaches 145°F / 63°C on an instant-read thermometer.

  3. 33

    Sear the Ground Beef Add the ground beef to the skillet, spreading it into an even layer and pressing it down firmly with the back of a spatula. Walk away for 3 full minutes. This is the most important step for flavor—resisting the urge to stir lets the beef develop a deep brown crust, the same crust that will later deglaze into your sauce. After the 3 minutes, break the beef apart with a wooden spoon and continue cooking, stirring occasionally, until no pink remains and it reaches an internal temperature of 160°F / 71°C on an instant-read thermometer—the USDA safe minimum for ground meat. Drain any excess fat if it looks more than a tablespoon or two; you want some fat for richness but not a greasy pool. **Chef Augustin’s Tip:** Use an instant-read thermometer, not the guess-and-hope method. Ground beef can look brown long before it actually hits 160°F, a phenomenon the USDA calls “premature browning.” I use a Thermapen ONE, which reads in one second, and I test in the center of the thickest clump. It’s the only way to be sure.

  4. 44

    Build the Sauce Pour in the crushed tomatoes and the 1/2 cup of water. Using a wooden spoon, scrape the bottom of the skillet vigorously to release every bit of the fond—those browned bits are pure savory depth. Stir in the salt and pepper, bring the sauce to a gentle simmer, and let it bubble for 3 minutes to marry the flavors. Fold in the thawed peas.

  5. 55

    Cook the Pasta Right in the Sauce Add the dried ziti directly to the skillet. Push the noodles down as best you can—they’ll stick out, and that’s fine. Cover the skillet with a tight-fitting lid or foil, reduce the heat to medium-low, and cook for 10–12 minutes. Stir once at the halfway mark to prevent clumping. At the end, the pasta should be just al dente with a slight bite, and the liquid should have thickened into a sauce that coats the tubes. If the mixture looks dry at 10 minutes, add another 2 tablespoons of water and cover for 2 more minutes. Cooking the pasta in the sauce (often called the “pasta risotto” method) is something Serious Eats has championed for its efficiency and the superior emulsion it creates. Because you’re not diluting the sauce with boiled pasta water, the final texture is creamier and the flavors more concentrated.

  6. 66

    Stir in the Ricotta Remove the skillet from the heat. Dollop the ricotta cheese across the surface and stir gently until it disappears into a creamy pink swirl throughout the pasta. This cools the mixture slightly so the mozzarella doesn’t immediately blitz when it hits the oven.

  7. 77

    Top with the Cheeses Sprinkle the mozzarella evenly over the entire surface, then shower the grated Parmesan on top. The Parmesan adds a salty, nutty crunch; the mozzarella gives you that irresistible cheese pull.

  8. 88

    Bake Until Bubbly Transfer the skillet to the preheated oven, uncovered. Bake for 10–12 minutes, until the cheese is fully melted, bubbling around the edges, and just starting to develop pale golden spots.

  9. 99

    Broil for the Golden Crust (Optional but Worth It) Switch the oven to broil. Broil for 1–2 minutes, watching like a hawk—the golden-brown, crisp-cheese moment arrives fast and can turn to char in seconds. The difference between “wow” and “fire alarm” is about 30 seconds.

  10. 1010

    Rest and Garnish Pull the skillet out and immediately scatter fresh basil leaves over the top. Let it rest for 5 minutes before you dig in. This isn’t just for show—letting it settle firms up the cheese and sauce so the portions hold together when you scoop them onto plates. It also gives you time to pour the wine and dim the lights.

Article and tips

There’s a moment about twenty minutes into making this dish when the kitchen starts to smell like a trattoria—onion and garlic sizzling in olive oil, ground beef hitting the hot pan with that deep, meaty hiss, and then the steam lifting off as you scrape the fond with crushed tomatoes. That’s the moment I know romantic dinner ideas for two](/recipes/romantic-dinner-ideas-for-two) is going to be good, even if date night is just the two of you in sweatpants on a Tuesday.

Most romantic dinner recipes ask you to dirty three pans, a baking dish, and a colander, and by the time you sit down you’re too tired to be charming. This one doesn’t. From sear to simmer to the final bubble of mozzarella under the broiler, everything happens in a single oven-safe skillet. The pasta cooks right in the sauce, soaking up beef-infused tomato flavor while releasing its starches to thicken everything into something velvety. There’s no water to drain, no extra bowl for the ricotta, and no casserole dish to wash. Yet the result looks and tastes like you spent the afternoon over the stove—which, honestly, you kind of didn’t.

Why This Works

The genius of this method is a technique pasta makers have used for centuries but home cooks often skip: cooking the dried pasta directly in the sauce. Instead of boiling ziti in a separate pot and then tossing it with sauce, you add the uncooked noodles straight into the healthy dinner recipes for 2 for 2 for 2 with a measured amount of liquid (tomato purée plus water) and let them absorb everything as they soften. The starch that leaches out isn’t lost down a drain; it stays right there, turning the sauce luscious and helping it cling to every tube.

I’ve tested this with both traditional boiled-then-baked ziti and the skillet method side by side. The skillet version was noticeably silkier—the sauce had a body that clung to the noodles in a way the drained pasta couldn’t match. The food science here is straightforward: pasta cooking liquid is essentially starch water, which is what chefs add to pan sauces to achieve that glossy, restaurant-quality finish. When you skip the colander, you capture every bit of that starch.

The second reason it works is the fond. When you sear easy week of meals in a hot skillet and let it sit undisturbed (a technique America’s Test Kitchen has shown repeatedly yields deeper browning), you build a layer of caramelized meat proteins on the bottom of the pan. Deglazing that fond with crushed tomatoes lifts all those savory bits into your sauce—no need for wine or stock. It’s the fastest path to a deeply flavored ragu that tastes like it simmered for hours, in roughly fifteen minutes.

A word on a few of these. Diamond Crystal kosher salt measures differently than Morton’s—Diamond Crystal’s flakes are larger and less dense, so if you’re using Morton’s, cut the volume by half. (This is why Cook’s Illustrated names Diamond Crystal their top pick; its flaky structure dissolves easy healthy dinner recipes for twoly and seasons evenly.) The crushed tomatoes are non-negotiable here. Diced won’t break down into the same velvety sauce, and purée is too smooth—you want some texture. If you only have whole canned tomatoes, crush them by hand in a bowl first.

The ricotta adds creaminess without the heaviness of heavy cream. If you can’t find whole-milk ricotta, part-skim works—just stir it thoroughly before using because it tends to separate more. Food52 notes that whole-milk ricotta has a richer mouthfeel because of its higher fat content, so drain part-skim for 10 minutes on a paper towel if the water pool bothers you. The dish will still be delicious.

Frozen peas are here because they add a pop of sweetness and color, but they’re also entirely optional. If you have frozen spinach, thaw and squeeze it dry, then stir it in instead. The recipe is forgiving like that.

What Most Recipes Get Wrong

The biggest mistake I see in baked ziti recipes is the assumption that you need to boil the pasta first. This creates three problems: you dirty a pot, you wash away the starch that would otherwise thicken your sauce, and you end up with overcooked noodles after the final bake. By the time that double-cooked ziti hits the table, it’s mushy.

The other common misstep is under-seasoning the sauce because “the cheese adds salt.” It doesn’t—at least, not enough. Mozzarella is mild, and Parmesan, while salty, is sprinkled on top. The sauce itself needs proper salting before the pasta goes in. Diamond Crystal kosher salt is forgiving because it’s less dense, so a 1/2 teaspoon is a safe starting point. Taste the sauce before adding the pasta and adjust.

Finally, many recipes call for layering the ingredients like a lasagna—meat sauce, noodles, ricotta, repeat—in a casserole dish. That looks pretty in photos but adds an extra dish and removes the sauce’s chance to thicken as the pasta cooks in it. The skillet method solves all of this in one piece of cookware.

Chef’s Tips & What I’ve Learned

  • Use a skillet with at least 2.5-inch high sides. When the pasta cooks, it absorbs liquid and expands, and a shallow skillet will overflow. A 10-inch cast-iron or stainless-steel skillet with straight sides works best; avoid slope-sided pans.
  • Pre-warm the ricotta. Cold ricotta straight from the fridge can seize when it hits the hot pasta sauce, making it lumpy. Take the ricotta out when you start cooking—it’ll reach room temperature by the time you need it.
  • Don’t skip the resting step. This is the one thing I learned after ruining the presentation of more than one skillet ziti. Slicing in too early means the sauce runs and the cheese slides. Five minutes of patience yields clean, restaurant-worthy portions.

  • Make it yours. This recipe is a canvas. Add a pinch of red pepper flakes with the garlic for heat. Stir in chopped frozen spinach with the peas for a vegetable boost. Swap the ground beef for Italian sausage (casing removed) for a punchy, fennel-spiked version. Every substitution works as long as you keep the liquid-to-pasta ratio roughly the same.

FAQ

Can I make this baked ziti ahead of time?

Yes, and it’s a lifesaver for a planned dessert for 2. Assemble the entire skillet through step 7 (cheeses on top), let it cool, then cover tightly and refrigerate for up to 24 hours. When you’re ready, let it sit at room temperature for 20 minutes, then bake at 375°F for 20–25 minutes. Because it starts colder, the center takes longer to heat, so insert an instant-read thermometer into the center of the dish—it should read 165°F before you serve, even though the beef was already cooked, as a reheated dish should reach that temperature for safety.

What wine pairs well with this dish?

A medium-bodied Italian red like Chianti Classico or Montepulciano d’Abruzzo has enough acidity to cut through the richness of the three cheeses and enough tannin to stand up to the beef ragu without overpowering it. If you prefer white wine, an unoaked Vermentino or dry Pinot Grigio with bright acidity works surprisingly well. I’ve served this with a $12 Banfi Chianti Superiore from the grocery store and it’s never felt like a compromise.

Can I use ground turkey instead of beef?

Absolutely, and it’s a common swap for those who don’t eat red meat. Substitute 8 ounces (225g) ground turkey directly. The key difference is that turkey is leaner—it won’t render fat the way beef does, so you’ll likely need an extra 1/2 tablespoon of olive oil to help it brown. And because the USDA sets the safe minimum temperature for ground poultry at 165°F (74°C), you’ll need to cook it slightly longer and use your thermometer to verify the higher target. The final dish will be lighter but still comforting.

How do I get that crispy golden cheese top without overcooking?

The broiler is your secret weapon, but it requires vigilance. Place the skillet in the upper-middle rack (about 6 inches from the element) and set a timer for 60 seconds. Open the oven door and check—if you see golden-brown spots, pull it. If not, give it 30 more seconds. Never step away during broiling; ovens can jump from golden to burnt in the time it takes to open a bottle of wine. Low-moisture mozzarella (sold in blocks or pre-shredded) browns better than fresh mozzarella, which contains too much water to crisp up.

What side dish goes well with this baked ziti?

This is a loaded one-dish meal, so I keep the side simple and bright. A handful of arugula dressed with lemon juice and a drizzle of olive oil—the peppery, acidic bite cuts through the richness of the cheese. If you want something warm, roasted frozen broccoli florets tossed in olive oil and salt and roasted at 400°F for 15 minutes are a zero-thought option. They take no prep and can roast while the ziti bakes.

Can I freeze leftovers?

Yes, and the leftovers are excellent. Portion the completely cooled ziti into an airtight container (I use glass meal-prep containers with snap lids) and freeze for up to 2 months. To reheat, thaw overnight in the refrigerator, then bake covered at 350°F for about 25 minutes, or microwave individual portions at 50% power in 90-second bursts to avoid separating the sauce. The pasta will soften a touch, but the beef and cheese hold their texture well.

It’s about a meal that brings you together at the end of the day, with almost no cleanup to interrupt the night. I’ve made this dish on actual Valentine’s Day and on a random Thursday in May. It fits both equally.
