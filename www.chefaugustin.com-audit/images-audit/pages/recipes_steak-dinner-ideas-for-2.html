Pan-Seared Steak Dinner for Two with Garlic Butter and Blistered Green Beans

Prep

Cook

Servings

2 servings

One skillet, one strip steak split between two plates, and a garlic butter that turns dinner into an occasion — here's exactly how I cook a steak dinner for two on a Tuesday.

By Chef Augustin LefèvreJuly 27, 2026· Updated August 5, 2026

Ingredients

  • New York strip steak (12-14 oz / 340-400g), at least 1.25 inches thick1
  • olive oil1 tablespoon
  • unsalted butter, plus 1 tablespoon for the sauce2 tablespoons
  • garlic, smashed2 cloves
  • sprigs fresh thyme or rosemary (optional)2
  • Diamond Crystal kosher salt, plus more for the beans1 teaspoon
  • freshly cracked black pepper1/2 teaspoon
  • fresh green beans, trimmed8 oz (225g)
  • beef stock, low-sodium1/4 cup (60ml)
  • Flaky sea salt, for finishing
Tips & FAQSave

Instructions

  1. 11

    Pull the steak from the fridge 30 minutes ahead and pat it bone-dry with paper towels. Season both sides generously with Diamond Crystal kosher salt and cracked black pepper.

  2. 22

    Heat a cast-iron skillet over high heat with 1 tablespoon olive oil until it shimmers and just begins to smoke, past 400°F / 205°C.

  3. 33

    Sear the steak undisturbed 3 minutes per side. USDA recommends a minimum internal temperature of 145°F / 63°C for whole-muscle beef; for medium-rare, pull the steak at 130°F / 54°C.

  4. 44

    Reduce to medium, add 2 tablespoons butter, smashed garlic, and herbs, and spoon the foaming butter over the steak for 60-90 seconds. Confirm 145°F / 63°C for safety or 130°F / 54°C for medium-rare.

  5. 55

    Transfer the steak to a board and rest, loosely tented, for a full 5 minutes as carryover cooking raises the temperature about 5°F.

  6. 66

    Return the skillet to medium-high with its garlic butter, add the green beans, and toss until charred in spots and bright green. Season with kosher salt.

  7. 77

    Push the beans aside, deglaze the hot pan with beef stock while scraping up the fond, reduce by half, then swirl in a final knob of butter off the heat until glossy.

  8. 88

    Slice the rested steak against the grain into 1/2-inch strips, divide between two warm plates, add the beans, and spoon the pan sauce over the top.

Article and tips

A single New York strip, cut thick and shared across two plates, is the smartest steak easy mexican dinner recipes recipes I know — because one good steak costs less than two mediocre ones and cooks more evenly than a pair of thin cuts crowding the pan. Every top result for this search either serves four (so you're stuck scaling down mid-cook) or hands you a plain grilled filet with no sauce and no plan for the sides. What almost none of them do is treat the whole dinner as one continuous move: sear the steak, blister the beans in the same fat, then build a pan sauce from the browned bits left behind. That's the angle here — one skillet, three components, zero wasted flavor.

You'll get a steak with a deep mahogany crust, green beans with charred edges and a squeaky-fresh center, and a two-tablespoon pan sauce that tastes like it took an hour. Total hands-on time is under half an hour, and most of that is you standing still while the steak rests.

Why This Works

A thick steak seared in a screaming-hot healthy dinner recipes for 2 for 2 develops flavor through the Maillard reaction — the cascade of chemical reactions between amino acids and sugars that kicks off above roughly 300°F (150°C). Thin steaks overcook before that crust forms; a single 1.25-inch strip gives you the surface area to brown hard while leaving a juicy interior. That's the whole case for buying one big steak instead of two small ones.

Cast iron is doing the heavy lifting. I've cooked this in both stainless steel and cast iron, and cast iron wins because it holds roughly 4× more thermal mass — when the cold steak hits the surface, the temperature barely dips, so the crust keeps building instead of stalling. Resting matters just as much as searing: as the steak sits off-heat, the muscle fibers relax and reabsorb juices that would otherwise flood your cutting board. Skip the rest and you lose the payoff of everything you just did.

The green beans and sauce ride the leftover fat and fond, so nothing you built in the pan goes to waste.

What Most Recipes Get Wrong

Most steak-dinner-for-two recipes tell you to cook two separate steaks. That's backwards. Two thin steaks fight for space in the pan, drop the surface temperature, and steam each other into grey submission. One thick steak sears clean, cooks evenly, and slices into an elegant fan across two plates. You also get one rest, one sauce, one skillet to wash.

The other quiet mistake is treating the sides as an afterthought cooked in a second pan. The fond left after searing — those dark browned bits welded to the skillet — is concentrated beef flavor. Cook your beans in it and build your sauce from it, and every component tastes like the steak. Reach for a second pan and you throw that away.

Chef's Tips & What I've Learned

A meat thermometer is worth more than any timer. The 3-3-3 rule (3 minutes per side, 3 minutes rest) is a decent starting estimate for a 1.25-inch steak, but pan heat, steak temperature, and your specific stove all vary — the thermometer is the only thing that tells the truth.

Warm your plates under hot tap water and dry them. A cold plate pulls heat out of a rested steak in seconds.

Chef Augustin's Tip: Save the garlic cloves from the basting step. Screamy creamy mashed potatoes for two and softened in butter, they're too good to toss — smear one onto a slice of bread while the steak rests. That's the cook's snack, and it's yours by right.

Salt the beans while they're still hot; seasoning clings to heat and moisture, and a cold bean shrugs it off.

What temperature should steak be for medium-rare?

For medium-rare, pull the steak from the heat at 130°F / 54°C and let it rest 5 minutes — carryover cooking will carry it to about 135°F. Note that the USDA recommends a minimum internal temperature of 145°F / 63°C for whole-muscle beef for safety. Medium-rare below that is a personal choice made with awareness of the guideline. Always measure with an instant-read thermometer inserted into the thickest part, not the edge.

What is the 3-3-3 steak rule?

The 3-3-3 rule is a simple guideline for thick-cut steaks: sear 3 minutes on the first side, 3 minutes on the second, then rest 3 minutes before slicing. It's a useful estimate for a steak around 1.25 inches thick cooked in a hot cast-iron skillet. That said, it's only an estimate — pan temperature and steak thickness change everything, so a meat thermometer reading 130°F / 54°C for medium-rare is the reliable way to nail doneness.

What sides go with a steak dinner for two?

The blistered green beans in this recipe cook in the same skillet, but steak plays well with plenty of options. Creamy mashed potatoes, oven-roasted baby red potatoes, and roasted asparagus are the classics. For a lighter plate, a sharp arugula salad cuts the richness of the butter. My rule for two people: pick one starch and one green, cook the green in the steak fat, and keep the starch simple so you're not juggling three pans on a easy lunch recipes.

Can I cook one steak for two people?

Yes — and I'd argue you should. A single thick-cut New York strip or ribeye (12 to 14 ounces) sears more evenly than two thin steaks and slices neatly into a shareable fan. You get one deep crust, one clean rest, and one pan sauce instead of two rushed steaks crowding the skillet. Slice against the grain into 1/2-inch strips and divide between two warm plates. It's cheaper than buying two cuts and, honestly, more elegant on the plate.

Do I need cast iron to make this?

Cast iron is ideal because it holds roughly 4× more heat than a thin stainless pan, so the surface temperature barely drops when the cold steak lands — that keeps the crust building instead of stalling. But a heavy stainless-steel skillet works if that's what you own; just preheat it a little longer and don't overcrowd it. Avoid nonstick for searing steak: it can't safely reach the high temperatures you need, and you lose the fond that makes the pan sauce.

Two plates, a fan of pink-centered slices under glossy sauce, the green beans still hissing faintly, and one softened garlic clove you'll fight over — that's the table you're setting tonight.
