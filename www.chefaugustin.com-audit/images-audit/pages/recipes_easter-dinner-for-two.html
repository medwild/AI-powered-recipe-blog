One-Sheet-Pan Easter Dinner for Two: Herb-Crusted Lamb Chops with Spring Vegetables

Ingredients

  • rack of lamb (8 ribs, about 1½ lbs / 680g), Frenched and trimmed1
  • olive oil, divided2 tablespoons
  • garlic, minced2 cloves
  • fresh rosemary, finely chopped1 tablespoon
  • fresh thyme leaves1 teaspoon
  • Diamond Crystal kosher salt½ teaspoon
  • black pepper, freshly ground½ teaspoon
  • baby new potatoes, halved½ lb (225g)
  • asparagus, trimmed½ lb (225g)
  • canned cherry tomatoes, drained well1 cup (150g)
  • red onion, cut into wedges1 small
  • unsalted butter, cold and cubed2 tablespoons
Tips & FAQSave

Instructions

  1. 11

    Preheat oven to 425°F (220°C) with rack in middle position. Line a large rimmed baking sheet with parchment paper.

  2. 22

    Pat lamb rack dry. Mix 1 tablespoon olive oil, minced garlic, rosemary, thyme, salt, and pepper. Rub all over lamb. Let rest at room temperature 15 minutes.

  3. 33

    Toss potatoes, asparagus, drained tomatoes, and onion wedges with remaining 1 tablespoon olive oil, a pinch of salt and pepper. Spread vegetables on prepared sheet pan, leaving space for the lamb.

  4. 44

    Place seasoned lamb rack in the center of the sheet pan, fat side up. Roast 20-25 minutes, or until lamb internal temperature reaches 130°F (54°C) for medium-rare. USDA safe minimum for lamb is 145°F (63°C); pulling at 130°F with 5-minute rest carries temperature to 145°F while preserving a pink center.

  5. 55

    Remove sheet pan from oven. Transfer lamb to a cutting board, tent loosely with foil, and rest 5 minutes. Internal temperature will rise to 145°F (63°C). Meanwhile, toss vegetables in the pan juices if desired.

  6. 66

    While lamb rests, place the sheet pan over two burners on medium-low heat. Scrape any browned bits and add cold butter cubes, swirling until melted and emulsified into a glossy sauce. Season with a pinch of salt.

  7. 77

    Slice lamb rack into individual chops between the bones. Divide between two plates alongside roasted vegetables and spoon the pan sauce over everything. Serve immediately.

Article and tips

Most Easter recipes for two make you choose between a three-hour ham that leaves a fridge full of leftovers or a pair of lamb chops that end up dry and lonely on an otherwise bare plate. I've done both. What actually works — what gets holiday elegance onto the table with a easy lunch recipes's worth of effort — is a single sheet pan, a well-rested rack of lamb, and the kind of spring vegetables that taste like they were roasted over an open fire instead of an apartment oven. This is that dinner.

The angle: every sheet-pan Easter recipe I found online either dumps raw ingredients together with no thought to cooking times, or skips the pan sauce entirely. You're left with something that looks fine but tastes like a rushed Tuesday. We're going to build the sauce right into the roasting process, using the lamb's rendered fat and the collapsed sweetness of burst cherry tomatoes to create a glossy, butter-finished pan sauce while the meat rests. No extra saucepan. No extra cleanup. Just the pan that's already in front of you.

Why This Works — The Food Science Angle

A rack of lamb is, generously, a $25 item. The goal is to treat it like one. The Maillard reaction — that brown, savory crust — develops fastest between 300°F and 500°F, and 425°F puts us right in the sweet spot where the exterior crisps before the interior hits medium-rare. I've tested this with both a thanksgiving dinner for two on its own and a sheet pan preheated with a cast-iron skillet. The sheet pan alone wins because it's lighter, and the healthy dinner ideas for two gain a better roast when they're not competing with a heat-sucking slab of metal.

But the real trick is the rest. Lamb, like any whole muscle, continues cooking after it leaves the oven. USDA recommends 145°F for safety, and that's what you'll get if you pull the rack at 130°F and let it rest for five minutes under foil. The temperature rises 10-15°F during those minutes, and the juices redistribute throughout the meat. Skip the rest, and you'll watch a pool of pink liquid drain onto your cutting board while you slice.

The roasted vegetables contribute more than just color. The onion wedges soften into jammy sweetness, the asparagus tips char slightly while the stems stay tender, and the canned shrimp — drained well so they don't steam — burst into concentrated flavor that forms the backbone of our pan sauce. Add cold butter at the end, and you emulsify the drippings into something that tastes like it spent an hour on the stove.

You pull the lamb off a hot tray and the fat-slicked juices are still bubbling. That's the moment you add butter. Cold fat hitting hot pan equals instant emulsion. No flour, no stock — just pure lamb essence.

What Most Recipes Get Wrong

Two things, universally. First, they don't tell you to rest the lamb, or they mention it in a single sentence at the end. Resting isn't optional; it's the most important 5-minute interval in this entire recipe. USDA temperature recommendations are achieved through carryover cooking during rest, not through blasting the meat to 145°F in the oven — which yields gym-sole toughness. Second, they ignore the pan sauce. You've just roasted meat and vegetables on a sheet pan; there's a quarter-inch of concentrated flavor sitting there. Butter, heat, 90 seconds — that's all it takes to turn it into something that makes the meal.

Lamb isn't steak. It doesn't need a screaming-hot sear. It needs a steady, even roast and a patient rest.

Chef's Tips & What I've Learned

Chef Augustin's Tip: I've made this with 12 different lamb racks over three spring seasons, and the single biggest variable beyond doneness is salt timing. Salt the lamb at least 15 minutes before it hits the oven, or up to 45 minutes if you have time. The salt dissolves some of the muscle proteins and helps retain moisture during roasting. Less than 10 minutes, and the salt just draws water to the surface without having time to reabsorb, leaving you with a wet exterior that resists browning.

Chef Augustin's Tip: If your asparagus is pencil-thin, add it to the pan 10 minutes into roasting; thick spears can go in at the start. The timing difference prevents thin asparagus from scorching into woody threads while the lamb finishes.

Here's something I learned the hard way: canned cherry tomatoes in juice, not puree. The puree-packed ones are too sweet and lack the acid punch needed to balance the lamb's richness. If you can only find whole peeled tomatoes, roughly chop two and drain them even more thoroughly.

FAQ

Can I prep this ahead of time?

Yes, and I do this often when I'm hosting. Season the lamb and arrange the dry vegetables on the sheet pan up to 6 hours in advance. Keep everything refrigerated separately, then bring the lamb to room temperature 15 minutes before roasting as directed. The olive oil will congeal in the fridge, which is fine — it loosens back up in the first few minutes of heat. Do not salt the vegetables ahead of time, or they'll release water and turn the pan into a steamer.

What if my oven runs hot or cold?

An oven thermometer costs less than $10 and will save you more than that in ruined lamb. I've worked in apartments where the oven ran 35 degrees hot; I set it to 425°F and the actual temperature was 460°F, which scorched the herb crust before the interior reached rare. Invest in a separate thermometer and adjust accordingly. For this recipe, the target baking temperature is consistently 425°F / 220°C. If your oven runs 25 degrees hot, set it to 400°F.

Can I swap the potatoes for another starch?

A half-pound of halved fingerling carrots or parsnips roasts just as well and leans further into the spring theme. I've also used Israeli couscous — tossed with the pan sauce after roasting — for a lighter option that soaks up every drop of those buttery juices. Just boil the couscous separately while the lamb roasts, drain, and fold it into the finished sauce on the stovetop step.

Is it safe to eat lamb medium-rare?

The USDA recommends cooking whole cuts of lamb to a minimum internal temperature of 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest for safety. In practice, pulling the lamb at 130°F (54°C) for medium-rare and resting it for 5 minutes reliably achieves a final temperature of 145°F. The carryover cooking during rest is what brings it into the safe zone. I always use a digital thermometer and rest for the full 5 minutes — no shortcuts.

What dessert goes well with this Easter dinner?

I keep it simple: a small lemon posset with fresh raspberries. It requires exactly three ingredients — heavy cream, sugar, and lemon juice — and sets in the fridge while you eat dinner. The bright acidity cuts through the richness of the lamb and butter sauce beautifully. If you're feeling ambitious, a two-serving carrot cake baked in a loaf pan works wonders and gives you the classic Easter dessert without a three-layer commitment.

The plates land on the table just as the last spring light is fading. There's a glass of Rhône red catching the candle flame, and the lamb chops are still faintly steaming. You've used one sheet pan, one cutting board, and exactly two plates. Easter dinner for two — actual dinner, not a project — and tomorrow morning you'll wake up without a sink full of roasting pans staring back at you.
