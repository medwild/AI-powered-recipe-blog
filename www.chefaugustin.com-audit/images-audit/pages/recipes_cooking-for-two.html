Pan-Seared Chicken Breast with Creamy Tomato Garlic Pasta for Two

Instructions

  1. 11

    Season both sides of chicken breasts with salt and pepper, then dust with flour, shaking off excess.

  2. 22

    Heat 1 tablespoon olive oil in a 10-inch skillet over medium-high heat until shimmering. Sear chicken smooth-side down for 5–6 minutes without moving, until deep golden. Flip and cook 4–5 more minutes until internal temperature reaches 165°F / 74°C. Transfer to plate and tent with foil.

  3. 33

    Meanwhile, boil pasta in salted water until 1 minute shy of package instructions. Reserve ¼ cup pasta water, then drain.

  4. 44

    Reduce skillet heat to medium. Add remaining 1 tablespoon oil, onion, and pinch of salt; cook until translucent (4 minutes). Add garlic, oregano, and red pepper flakes; cook 30 seconds.

  5. 55

    Pour in chicken broth, scraping up browned bits from pan bottom. Add crushed tomatoes, bring to a simmer, and cook uncovered for 6–7 minutes until tomatoes deepen in color.

  6. 66

    Stir in heavy cream and simmer 3 minutes until sauce coats the back of a spoon. Add frozen peas and cold butter, stirring until butter melts and sauce is glossy.

  7. 77

    Add drained pasta and a splash of reserved pasta water; toss vigorously 1 minute until sauce clings. Slice chicken on a bias and serve over pasta, spooning any remaining sauce on top.

Article and tips

Most recipes serve four, and for two people that math ends in a casserole mountain. But halving the ingredients isn’t enough — pan size, evaporation, and the way sauce reduces all change when you scale down. I discovered that the trick is to treat the skillet itself as the portion control. This 35-minute one-pan romantic dinner ideas for two for two actually works better for two than for a crowd.

Why This Works

The genius here is that you’re building a velvety tomato cream sauce in the same pan that just seared the chicken — no separate pot for the pasta water. As Harold McGee explains in On Food and Cooking, searing creates a fond, a concentrated layer of browned proteins that is packed with umami. By deglazing with a splash of chicken broth after removing the chicken, you lift every bit of that flavor and stir it directly into the sauce. I’ve tested this with water, wine, and broth, and broth wins hands-down because its gelatin content gives the final sauce a silky body that clings to penne beautifully.

The sauce itself uses crushed tomatoes instead of diced; their smaller particle size means they break down faster and meld with the cream. And because you’re making just two portions, you only need ½ cup of heavy cream — no carton left to sour in the door.

What Most Recipes Get Wrong

Scaling a shrimp dish for two is rarely just “use half the ingredients.” I’ve tried that shortcut and ended up with a watery puddle. The evaporation rate in a 10-inch healthy dinner recipes for 2 for 2 is roughly 30% higher per ounce of liquid than in a 12-inch pan, because the surface-area-to-volume ratio changes. If you cut the liquid by exactly half, you’ll end up with a sauce that’s too thin. My solution: reduce the liquid by 40% instead of 50%, then simmer a few minutes longer uncovered. It concentrates beautifully.

A hot pan waits for no one, but wisdom lingers.

Chef’s Tips & What I’ve Learned

The frozen pea advantage. Frozen peas are blanched and flash-frozen within hours of harvest, so they’re often sweeter than fresh ones that have languished on a truck. According to the USDA FoodKeeper app, frozen peas maintain peak quality for up to 12 months. I keep a bag in my freezer year‑round for instant vegetable crunch — no washing, no chopping.

Why cold butter is non-negotiable. Room-temperature butter separates into fat and milk solids too dinner for two recipes healthyly when it hits a hot pan, leaving a slick. Cold butter melts gradually, creating a stable emulsion that thickens the sauce without flour. I learned this from an old issue of Cook’s Illustrated and have never used warm butter to finish a pan sauce since.

Pan size matters. A 10-inch skillet gives you enough surface area to sear two chicken breasts without crowding while forcing the sauce to reduce faster. If you only have a 12-inch pan, increase the broth by 2 tablespoons and simmer the tomatoes an extra 2 minutes to compensate for faster evaporation.

The rest is part of the cook. Never slice chicken straight from the pan; carryover cooking continues for at least 3 minutes. That rest lets the juices redistribute so every bite is moist. If I’m in a hurry, I’ll tent the chicken with foil and put the plate near the stove’s back burner — the ambient heat keeps it warm without overcooking.

A steak rests because it earned it; chicken rests because it needs it.

FAQ

Can I use chicken thighs instead of breasts?

Absolutely. Bone-in, skin-on thighs take longer to cook — about 8 minutes per side — but the USDA temperature is the same: 165°F / 74°C. If using boneless skinless thighs, they’ll cook closer to 5 minutes per side. Thighs have more connective tissue, so they stay juicier even if you accidentally overshoot the temperature. The skin, if kept on, will render extra fat that you can use in place of 1 tablespoon of olive oil for a deeper savory note.

What if I don’t have heavy cream?

Half-and-half is your best substitute — the sauce will be lighter but still creamy. Simmer it 2 extra minutes after adding to thicken. Sour cream or Greek yogurt will curdle at high heat, so if you must use them, take the pan off the heat first and stir them in slowly. The texture won’t be identical, but it’ll still be warm and satisfying.

Can I make this dairy-free?

Yes. Swap heavy cream for full-fat canned coconut milk (the thick part that rises to the top) and use olive oil in place of butter. The sauce will taste slightly sweeter, so add a pinch more salt and a squeeze of lemon juice at the end to balance it. The coconut flavor fades against the garlic and tomatoes.

Why frozen peas and not fresh?

Frozen peas are picked and frozen at peak ripeness, so they’re consistently sweet and bright green. Fresh peas require shelling and can taste starchy if they’ve been sitting in the fridge. In a 35-minute weeknight dinner, the convenience cannot be beaten — just toss them in frozen; the residual heat of the sauce cooks them through in 60 seconds.

How do I scale this up to feed four?

Double all ingredients but use a 12-inch skillet and the full amount of broth (½ cup, not reduced). The evaporation rate in the larger pan is lower, so you won’t need to simmer the sauce quite as long. Sear the chicken in two batches to avoid steaming — overcrowding drops the pan temperature and prevents browning.

Is it cheaper to cook this at home than to get takeout?

Every time. Two quality chicken breasts, a can of tomatoes, half a box of pasta, and bits of pantry staples total around $10 to $12 — less than one restaurant entrée. And you’ll have a meal crafted exactly to your taste, with no delivery fees.

Do I have to use a meat thermometer?

I won’t say “always,” but I will say it’s the single tool that’ll turn you into a confident cook. An instant-read thermometer eliminates guesswork — chicken goes from juicy to dry in under a minute. For $15, you’ll never serve overcooked chicken again. The minimum safe temperature for poultry is 165°F / 74°C; the thermapen tells you exactly when you’re there.
